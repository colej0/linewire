Why closing line value predicts long-term results

Win-loss records are noisy. Over 1,000 bets at −110, a bettor with no edge will land anywhere from about 47% to 53% wins about 95% of the time, a range wider than most real edges. The closing line is a better yardstick: it is the price after the market has absorbed injury news, weather and the biggest bets, so it is the sharpest public estimate of the true chance. Beat it consistently and you are usually betting positive expected value, whether or not this week’s results show it. That is why sharp bettors track CLV, and why books watch it when they decide who to limit.

Measure against the no-vig close when you can. The posted closing price includes the book’s margin, which inflates the implied chance on your side and so overstates your edge; removing the vig from both sides gives the fair price. Cents are the simplest scale (−110 to −130 is 20 cents; +105 to −105 is 10), and implied-probability points let you compare favorites and underdogs. FAQ

What is closing line value (CLV)?

Closing line value is how much better your price was than the final price before the game started. Bet a team at −110 that closes −130 (other side +110) and you beat the close by 20 cents. Against the no-vig close that is about a 3.6% edge, because the close is the market's best estimate of the true chance.

Why does CLV predict long-term results?

The closing line has absorbed nearly all the information and money on a game, so it is the sharpest public estimate of the true probability. If you regularly get better prices than the close, you are usually getting positive expected value, and CLV shows that after a few hundred bets, long before win-loss records separate skill from luck.

How do you calculate CLV in percent?

Remove the vig from the close: divide each side's implied probability by their total to get the fair probability. Your edge is your decimal odds × the fair closing probability − 1. At −110 (1.909) against a fair close of 54.3%, that is 1.909 × 0.543 − 1 ≈ +3.6%.
